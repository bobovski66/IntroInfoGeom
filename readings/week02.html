Week 2 · Reading

Payoff Matrices, Competition, and Stability

Last week, we learned to analyze a replicator equation when its fitness functions were already given. Now we will generate those functions from interactions, then predict the entire phase portrait directly from four numbers.

Payoff-matrix explorerGuided labQuiz
Learning goals

Read a \(2\times2\) payoff matrix; form expected fitness functions; classify dominance, coexistence, and bistability; use the phase line and linearization to explain stability; and discover how parameters change a dynamical system.

1. Interactions determine fitness

In the two-strategy replicator equation, \(x\) is the proportion of type A, while \(1-x\) is the proportion of type B. We have

\[\boxed{x'=x(1-x)\bigl(f_A(x)-f_B(x)\bigr).}\]

In Week 1 we prescribed the functions \(f_A\) and \(f_B\). Suppose instead that the growth or success of an individual depends on which type it encounters. We describe one encounter with a payoff matrix:

\[M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}.\]

The row tells us which type receives the payoff; the column tells us which opponent it encounters. This is a mathematical convention, not a moral evaluation of an individual or a population.

Type receiving payoffAgainst AAgainst B
A\(a\)\(b\)
B\(c\)\(d\)

If opponents are sampled randomly from a well-mixed population, an A individual meets A with probability \(x\) and B with probability \(1-x\). The expected payoff is therefore a weighted average:

\[\begin{aligned}f_A(x)&=ax+b(1-x),\\f_B(x)&=cx+d(1-x).\end{aligned}\]

For this model, we take those expected payoffs as the fitnesses that determine changes in population proportions. The matrix determines the differential equation.

2. One matrix produces our equation from Week 1

Consider the matrix

\[M=\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\3&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The first row says that A receives payoff 2 when meeting A and payoff 4 when meeting B. The second says B receives payoff 3 when meeting A and payoff 1 when meeting B. The expected fitnesses are

\[\begin{aligned}f_A(x)&=2x+4(1-x)=4-2x,\\f_B(x)&=3x+1(1-x)=1+2x.\end{aligned}\]

The fitness difference is \(f_A-f_B=3-4x\). Therefore

\[\boxed{x'=x(1-x)(3-4x).}\]

Exactly the equation we studied in Week 1! Its interior equilibrium is \(x^*=3/4\), and the fitness advantage changes from positive to negative at that point. This is a model of stable coexistence: neither type takes over completely from an interior initial state.

Here is the important change in perspective: the population's dynamics now follow from four parameters \(a,b,c,d\), rather than from a differential equation chosen in advance.

3. Reduce the matrix to a line

Subtract the expected fitnesses for a general matrix:

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta(x)&=f_A(x)-f_B(x)\\&=(a-c)x+(b-d)(1-x)\\&=(b-d)+(a-b-c+d)x.\end{aligned}\]

Thus every two-strategy payoff matrix yields

\[\boxed{x'=x(1-x)\left[(b-d)+(a-b-c+d)x\right].}\]

Although this is a cubic ODE, its sign inside \(0<x<1\) is controlled entirely by the straight line \(\Delta(x)\). The factors \(x\) and \(1-x\) are positive in the interior. We can classify the phase portrait by studying a line!

At the endpoints, the advantages are especially easy to interpret:

\(\Delta(0)=b-d\)

If A is rare, it mostly meets B. A increases from rarity when \(b>d\).

\(\Delta(1)=a-c\)

If B is rare, it mostly meets A. B increases from rarity when \(c>a\).

These conditions allow us to determine whether either type can invade a population composed almost entirely of its competitor.

An interior equilibrium exists when the advantage line crosses zero:

\[x^*=\frac{d-b}{a-b-c+d},\qquad 0<x^*<1.\]

This formula applies when the denominator is nonzero and the resulting value belongs to the open interval. If \(\Delta(x)\equiv0\), every point is an equilibrium; if the line has no interior root, the only equilibria are at the endpoints.

4. Four phase portraits from four numbers

Let \(\Delta_0=\Delta(0)=b-d\) and \(\Delta_1=\Delta(1)=a-c\). When neither is zero, the signs at the two endpoints determine the qualitative behavior.

SignsBehaviorAttracting state(s)
\(\Delta_0>0,\ \Delta_1>0\)A dominates\(x=1\)
\(\Delta_0<0,\ \Delta_1<0\)B dominates\(x=0\)
\(\Delta_0>0,\ \Delta_1<0\)Stable coexistenceInterior \(x^*\)
\(\Delta_0<0,\ \Delta_1>0\)Bistability / coordination\(x=0\) and \(x=1\)

In a coordination model, each strategy does relatively better when it is common. An unstable interior state separates two basins of attraction: the initial proportion determines which type eventually dominates.

Worked example: coordination

Consider \(M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}f_A(x)&=4x+(1-x)=1+3x,\\f_B(x)&=2x+3(1-x)=3-x,\\\Delta(x)&=-2+4x.\end{aligned}\]

Therefore \(x'=x(1-x)(-2+4x)\). The interior equilibrium is \(x^*=1/2\). Solutions starting below \(1/2\) decrease toward 0, while those starting above \(1/2\) increase toward 1. The equilibrium at \(1/2\) is a threshold, not an attractor.

Notice: The interior equilibrium of stable coexistence and the interior equilibrium of bistability are both zeros of \(\Delta\). Their difference is not their location but which way the arrows point on either side.

5. Linearization gives a local stability test

Write \(\alpha=b-d\) and \(\beta=a-b-c+d\). Then

\[F(x)=x(1-x)(\alpha+\beta x).\]

For an interior equilibrium \(x^*=-\alpha/\beta\), the third factor vanishes. When we differentiate the product and evaluate at \(x^*\), only one term remains:

\[\boxed{F'(x^*)=x^*(1-x^*)\beta.}\]

Because the first two factors are positive in the interior, the sign of \(F'(x^*)\) is the sign of \(\beta\). Negative \(\beta\) produces stable coexistence; positive \(\beta\) produces an unstable threshold. Near a hyperbolic equilibrium, perturbations \(u=x-x^*\) satisfy approximately

\[u'=F'(x^*)u,\qquad u(t)\approx u(0)e^{F'(x^*)t}.\]

We can test the endpoints just as easily:

\[F'(0)=b-d=\Delta_0,\qquad F'(1)=c-a=-\Delta_1.\]

A negative derivative at an endpoint indicates attraction from within the population interval; a positive derivative indicates repulsion. When one of these derivatives is zero, the usual linearization test is inconclusive and the phase line must be inspected directly.

6. Changing a payoff changes the phase portrait

Return to the coexistence matrix and leave \(b=4,\ c=3,\ d=1\) fixed while varying \(a\):

\[M(a)=\begin{pmatrix}a&4\\3&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The fitness difference simplifies to

\[\Delta(x)=3+(a-6)x.\]

If \(a<3\), then \(\Delta(0)=3>0\) and \(\Delta(1)=a-3<0\), so the model has stable coexistence. At \(a=3\), the interior equilibrium reaches the endpoint \(x=1\). If \(a>3\), both endpoint advantages are positive and A dominates. This is a bifurcation: a parameter change alters the qualitative phase portrait.

Use the demo to move \(a\) through 3 and watch the intersection move to the boundary. At the critical parameter, the endpoint is nonhyperbolic: the linearization at \(x=1\) is zero, yet a sign chart still shows that interior solutions approach 1.

7. Not every payoff change changes the trajectories

Payoffs are not absolute units of population size. Only fitness differences enter the replicator equation.

If we add the same constant \(k\) to all four entries of \(M\), then both expected fitnesses increase by \(k\), so their difference—and the replicator equation—remain unchanged.

\[M\longmapsto M+k\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\implies\quad \Delta(x)\text{ unchanged}.\]

Multiplying every payoff by a positive constant \(\lambda\), on the other hand, multiplies \(x'\) by \(\lambda\). This changes the speed at which a trajectory is followed, but not its direction or equilibrium set. This is a useful distinction between the geometry of the trajectories and their time parametrization.
