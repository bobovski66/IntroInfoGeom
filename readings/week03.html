Three Strategies and the Probability Simplex

A two-strategy population lives on an interval. Add a third strategy and its state space becomes a triangle. We can still use our differential equations tools—but a phase line becomes a phase plane, and circulating trajectories become possible.

Simplex explorerGuided labQuiz
Learning goals

Read population states as points in a triangle; derive and analyze a three-strategy replicator system; explain why its edges are invariant; interpret cyclic competition, closed orbits, and spirals; and connect stability to a conserved or monotone quantity.

1. From an interval to a triangle

In the first two weeks, a population had two strategies. Once we specified the proportion \(x\) of A, the proportion of B was \(1-x\). Thus one number described the entire composition, and the state space was the interval \([0,1]\).

With three strategies A, B, and C, let

\[\mathbf p=(p_A,p_B,p_C),\qquad p_A+p_B+p_C=1,\qquad p_i\geq0.\]

The three components are nonnegative proportions summing to one. Knowing two determines the third: \(p_C=1-p_A-p_B\). This is a two-dimensional state space, even though we use three coordinates.

The triangle is called the standard 2-simplex, written \(\Delta^2\). Its interior consists of populations containing all three strategies. Its edges correspond to populations where exactly one strategy is absent, and its vertices to populations with a single type.

Interval, \(\Delta^1\)

Two strategies, one independent proportion, and a one-dimensional phase line.

Triangle, \(\Delta^2\)

Three strategies, two independent proportions, and a two-dimensional phase portrait.

2. Derive the three-strategy replicator system

Let \(N_i\) denote the population of type \(i\), and suppose \(N_i'=f_i(\mathbf p)N_i\). Set \(N=N_A+N_B+N_C\) and \(p_i=N_i/N\). The total population obeys

\[N'=\sum_jf_jN_j=N\sum_jp_jf_j.\]

The weighted average \(\overline f=\sum_jp_jf_j\) is the mean fitness of the population. Using the quotient rule once more gives

\[\begin{aligned}p_i'&=\frac{N_i'N-N_iN'}{N^2}\\&=p_i\left(f_i(\mathbf p)-\overline f(\mathbf p)\right).\end{aligned}\]
Replicator equations for three strategies\[\boxed{p_i'=p_i(f_i-\overline f),\qquad \overline f=p_Af_A+p_Bf_B+p_Cf_C.}\]

Every strategy changes in proportion to its advantage over the population average. A fitness of 2 does not, on its own, tell us whether a strategy is gaining share: we must compare it with \(\overline f\).

Worked example: a velocity at one state

Suppose \(\mathbf p=(0.2,0.3,0.5)\) and the current fitnesses are \(\mathbf f=(3,1,2)\). Then

\[\overline f=0.2(3)+0.3(1)+0.5(2)=1.9.\]

The velocity is

\[\boxed{\mathbf p'=(0.2(3-1.9),\,0.3(1-1.9),\,0.5(2-1.9))=(0.22,-0.27,0.05).}\]

A and C gain share while B loses share, and \(0.22-0.27+0.05=0\), as normalization requires.

3. Why the triangle and its edges are invariant

Differentiate the sum of proportions along a trajectory:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dt}(p_A+p_B+p_C)&=\sum_i p_i(f_i-\overline f)\\&=\overline f-\overline f(p_A+p_B+p_C)=0.\end{aligned}\]

Thus the velocity lies in the plane \(v_A+v_B+v_C=0\), tangent to the affine plane containing the simplex. This is the multivariable version of having solutions stay inside the interval in Week 1.

There is a stronger fact. When a strategy is absent, \(p_k=0\), its equation becomes \(p_k'=0\). For smooth fitness functions, uniqueness implies that a trajectory beginning on that edge remains there. Each edge carries its own two-strategy replicator dynamics.

\[p_k(t)=p_k(0)\exp\left(\int_0^t(f_k-\overline f)\,d\tau\right).\]

This identity shows that a positive proportion stays positive at every finite time at which the solution exists. An interior trajectory can approach an edge asymptotically, but it cannot arrive there in finite time. The vertices are equilibria because two proportions vanish there.

Try this geometrically: Put the initial point exactly on an edge in the demo. Watch the trajectory move along that edge. Then place the point just inside the triangle. Does it ever cross onto the edge?

4. A payoff matrix for three strategies

We can represent interactions exactly as in Week 2, except the matrix is now \(3\times3\). If \(M=(m_{ij})\) gives the payoff to row strategy \(i\) against column strategy \(j\), then

\[\mathbf f(\mathbf p)=M\mathbf p,\qquad f_i=\sum_jm_{ij}p_j.\]

In the following model, A outperforms B, B outperforms C, and C outperforms A. The payoff for a win is \(+1\), a loss is \(-s\), and a match with one's own strategy has payoff zero:

\[\boxed{M_s=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-s\\-s&0&1\\1&-s&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad s>0.}\]

The fitnesses become

\[\begin{aligned}f_A&=p_B-sp_C,\\f_B&=p_C-sp_A,\\f_C&=p_A-sp_B.\end{aligned}\]

The point \(\mathbf p^*=(1/3,1/3,1/3)\) is always an equilibrium: there, all three fitnesses are equal. But what happens near this point depends on \(s\). The same triangle can support very different phase portraits.

5. Circulation, inward spirals, and outward spirals

Our familiar two-strategy systems moved either toward or away from equilibrium along a line. In a two-dimensional system, a trajectory can circulate as it approaches or leaves an equilibrium. Cyclic competition is an especially transparent example.

Loss magnitude \(s<1\)The center attracts; trajectories spiral inward toward equal proportions.
Balanced case \(s=1\)Interior trajectories form closed periodic orbits around the center.
Loss magnitude \(s>1\)The center repels; trajectories move outward toward the boundary in successive cycles.

At \(s=1\), the matrix is antisymmetric, so \(\mathbf p^TM_1\mathbf p=0\) for every state. The mean payoff is zero, but the individual strategy proportions usually continue moving. Equal average payoff does not mean each strategy is stationary.

The outward case does not mean the state crosses the boundary, nor that it immediately settles at a single vertex. It can spend successively longer periods near the boundary while the predominant type keeps changing.

6. A conserved quantity explains the closed curves

How could we tell whether a trajectory circulates at a fixed distance from the center, or spirals inward or outward? For this model, consider the product

\[P(\mathbf p)=p_Ap_Bp_C.\]

In the interior, differentiate its logarithm using the replicator equations:

\[\frac{d}{dt}\ln P=\sum_i\frac{p_i'}{p_i}=\sum_i f_i-3\overline f.\]

For the cyclic payoff matrix, if \(Q=p_Ap_B+p_Bp_C+p_Cp_A\), then \(\sum_i f_i=1-s\) and \(\overline f=(1-s)Q\). Hence

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\ln P=(1-s)(1-3Q).}\]

Since \(Q\leq1/3\) for any three probabilities summing to one, with equality only at the center, the second factor is nonnegative. Therefore:

If \(s<1\)

\(P\) increases along nonstationary interior trajectories, toward its maximum \(1/27\) at the center.

If \(s>1\)

\(P\) decreases along nonstationary interior trajectories, indicating movement toward the simplex boundary.

At \(s=1\), the logarithmic derivative is zero and the product is conserved. Its interior level curves are closed loops surrounding the center. Because the vector field is nonzero on those loops, the flow follows them periodically.

Notice that \(P\) is not Shannon entropy: it is an elementary scalar function whose behavior distinguishes three phase portraits. Later, we will use information-theoretic functions for a similar stability analysis.

7. Linearization in two independent coordinates

Set \(x=p_A\), \(y=p_B\), and \(p_C=1-x-y\). Substituting the cyclic fitnesses gives a planar system

\[\begin{aligned}x'&=x(f_A-\overline f),\\y'&=y(f_B-\overline f).\end{aligned}\]

Now the methods of a first course in systems of ODEs apply: equilibria, the Jacobian matrix, eigenvalues, and phase portraits. At the central equilibrium, the Jacobian is

\[J_s=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}s&s+1\\-(s+1)&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Its eigenvalues are

\[\boxed{\lambda_\pm=\frac{s-1}{6}\pm i\,\frac{\sqrt3(s+1)}6.}\]

This gives exactly the behavior seen in our plots: a stable spiral for \(s<1\), a linear center for \(s=1\), and an unstable spiral for \(s>1\). At \(s=1\), linearization alone does not prove that the nonlinear trajectories are closed; the conserved product \(P\) supplies that additional information.

Optional calculation: the determinant and trace

You can verify that \(\operatorname{tr}J_s=(s-1)/3\) and \(\det J_s=(s^2+s+1)/9>0\). The eigenvalues have nonzero imaginary parts for \(s>0\), and the trace determines whether small oscillations are damped or amplified.

The passage to the simplex shows why we needed three population proportions but only two independent coordinates. The geometry of the state space and the dimensionality of the dynamical system agree.

8. Toward probability distributions

A normalized state \(\mathbf p=(p_A,p_B,p_C)\) describes the frequencies of three strategies. It is also a probability distribution: if we sample one individual at random, \(p_A\) is the chance of encountering A, and similarly for B and C.

The replicator system therefore gives us a curve of probability distributions \(\mathbf p(t)\). Its velocity is tangent to the simplex, its boundary has a dynamical meaning, and its trajectories can have invariant quantities. In Week 4, we will study these probability distributions as mathematical objects in their own right, even without specifying a population model.
