Entropy, Log-Odds, and Exponential Updating

Information theory assigns numerical quantities to individual outcomes and to entire probability distributions. We develop surprisal and Shannon entropy, then use log-odds to connect information-theoretic coordinates with replicator dynamics.

Chapter PDFInformation explorerGuided labQuiz
Learning goals

Interpret surprisal and Shannon entropy, calculate them in nats and bits, differentiate Bernoulli entropy, connect replicator dynamics to log-odds, derive exponential normalization for constant fitness, and distinguish increasing fitness from increasing entropy.

1. How surprising is an outcome?

A distribution specifies probabilities for possible outcomes. Some outcomes are common; others are rare. We would like an information measure that is larger when an event was less likely, equals zero for an event that was certain, and adds when we observe independent events together.

The function satisfying these natural requirements, up to a positive multiplicative constant, is the negative logarithm:

\[\boxed{I(E)=-\ln P(E),\qquad P(E)>0.}\]

The quantity \(I(E)\) is called the surprisal or self-information of the realized event. With natural logarithms, its unit is the nat.

For an event of probability \(1/2\), surprisal is \(\ln2\) nats. For probability \(1/10\), it is \(\ln10\) nats, a much larger number. As probability approaches zero from above, surprisal grows without bound. An impossible event with probability zero is never sampled under that model; its formal surprisal is infinite.

Why logarithms? For two independent events A and B, \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}I(A\cap B)&=-\ln(P(A)P(B))\\&=-\ln P(A)-\ln P(B)\\&=I(A)+I(B).\end{aligned}\]

Multiplication of independent probabilities becomes addition of information. This is the central algebraic reason logarithms appear throughout information theory.

2. Shannon entropy is expected surprisal

A particular observation carries surprisal, but a probability distribution has an average expected surprisal. For possible outcomes \(1,\ldots,n\) with probabilities \(\mathbf p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\), define

Shannon entropy\[\boxed{H(\mathbf p)=-\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\ln p_i.}\]

The convention \(0\ln0=0\) is defined by continuity. This makes entropy meaningful even when some outcomes have probability zero. Entropy is a function of the distribution, not of the particular sample drawn from it.

For a fair coin, both outcomes have probability \(1/2\), so \(H=\ln2\) nats. For a three-outcome uniform distribution, \(H=\ln3\) nats. A deterministic distribution such as \((1,0,0)\) has entropy zero.

Nats

Using natural logarithms: \(H_{\mathrm{nat}}=-\sum p_i\ln p_i\).

Bits

Using base-2 logarithms: \(H_{\mathrm{bit}}=-\sum p_i\log_2p_i=H_{\mathrm{nat}}/\ln2\).

Worked example: uncertainty in three outcomes

For \(P=(1/2,1/3,1/6)\),

\[\begin{aligned}H(P)&=-\frac12\ln\frac12-\frac13\ln\frac13-\frac16\ln\frac16\\&\approx1.0114\text{ nats}\\&\approx1.4591\text{ bits}.\end{aligned}\]

The maximum entropy among three-outcome distributions is \(\ln3\approx1.0986\) nats, reached at equal probabilities. P is uncertain, but not maximally uncertain.

3. Binary entropy is a calculus problem

For a Bernoulli distribution \(P_p=(p,1-p)\), Shannon entropy becomes a one-variable function:

\[\boxed{H(p)=-p\ln p-(1-p)\ln(1-p).}\]

Differentiate using the product and chain rules:

\[\begin{aligned}H'(p)&=-(\ln p+1)+(\ln(1-p)+1)\\&=\ln\frac{1-p}{p},\\ H''(p)&=-\frac1p-\frac1{1-p}=-\frac1{p(1-p)}.\end{aligned}\]

Because \(H''(p)\) is negative throughout the interior, \(H\) is strictly concave. Its only interior critical point is \(p=1/2\), where \(H=\ln2\). This is the unique maximum.

Notice that \(H'(p)\) is the negative of the log-odds of outcome 1. The two concepts will meet in the next section.

For three or more categories, entropy is likewise maximized at the uniform distribution. A multivariable-calculus proof can use a Lagrange multiplier for \(\sum p_i=1\): setting \(-(\ln p_i+1)=\lambda\) shows all positive coordinates agree; concavity gives the global maximum \(H=\ln n\).

4. Log-odds transform a replicator equation

Return to Week 1. Let \(x(t)\) be the proportion of strategy A, and let \(\Delta(x)=f_A(x)-f_B(x)\) be its fitness advantage:

\[x'=x(1-x)\Delta(x).\]

For an interior state \(0<x<1\), define the odds of A to B by \(x/(1-x)\), and the log-odds by

\[L(x)=\ln\frac{x}{1-x}.\]

Differentiating along a solution gives:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dL}{dt}&=\left(\frac1x+\frac1{1-x}\right)x'\\&=\frac{x'}{x(1-x)}\\&=\boxed{f_A-f_B.}\end{aligned}\]

Every two-strategy replicator system satisfies this identity for interior trajectories. The log-odds increase at exactly the fitness advantage. If that advantage is constant, log-odds change linearly in time.

\[\Delta(x)=k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L(t)=L(0)+kt.\]

Solve this expression for \(x(t)\):

\[\boxed{x(t)=\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{1-x_0}{x_0}\right)e^{-kt}}.}\]

The logistic function from Week 1 has returned. In ordinary proportion coordinates, the trajectory is curved; in log-odds coordinates it is a straight line. That is an early example of how a good coordinate system reveals the structure of a differential equation.

5. Three or more strategies: exponential normalization

For three strategies the replicator equation is \(p_i'=p_i(f_i-\bar f)\). Suppose the fitness values \(f_i\) are constant in time and do not depend on the population composition. Consider the positive weights

\[w_i(t)=p_i(0)e^{f_it},\qquad Z(t)=\sum_jw_j(t).\]

Define a normalized distribution by \(p_i(t)=w_i(t)/Z(t)\). Differentiating gives

\[\begin{aligned}p_i'&=\frac{f_iw_iZ-w_iZ'}{Z^2}\\&=p_i\left(f_i-\frac{Z'}Z\right)=p_i(f_i-\bar f),\end{aligned}\]

because \(Z'/Z=\sum_jp_jf_j=\bar f\). This yields the exact solution:

Constant-fitness replicator solution\[\boxed{p_i(t)=\frac{p_i(0)e^{f_it}}{\sum_jp_j(0)e^{f_jt}}.}\]

The probabilities stay nonnegative and continue to sum to one automatically. This is exponential weighting followed by normalization, closely related to the softmax operation used in statistics and machine learning. With positive initial probabilities, it can be written as the softmax of the scores \(\ln p_i(0)+f_it\).

For two strategies, division of the two formulas yields another useful relation:

\[\ln\frac{p_i(t)}{p_j(t)}=\ln\frac{p_i(0)}{p_j(0)}+(f_i-f_j)t.\]

Differences in fitness become constant velocities in logarithmic probability ratios. Adding the same constant to every \(f_i\) changes all unnormalized weights by the same factor but cancels during normalization.

Important distinction: This explicit softmax formula uses constant fitness values. When fitness depends on the current state, the log-ratio derivative identity remains valid, but the straight-line solution generally does not.

6. Entropy does not always increase

Dynamical stability describes trajectories near an equilibrium. Shannon entropy measures the uncertainty of an outcome sampled from a distribution. Stability therefore does not determine whether entropy increases or decreases.

For the Bernoulli entropy, along any differentiable proportion curve \(x(t)\), the chain rule gives

\[\boxed{\frac{dH}{dt}=H'(x)x'=\ln\frac{1-x}{x}\,x(1-x)\Delta(x).}\]

The sign depends on both the location \(x\) relative to \(1/2\) and the direction of motion. Even a trajectory heading toward a stable equilibrium can have entropy increasing at first and decreasing later.

For our familiar equation \(x'=x(1-x)(3-4x)\), an initial state \(x_0=0.1\) increases toward \(3/4\). While \(x\) moves from 0.1 to 0.5, entropy increases. After \(x\) passes 0.5, entropy decreases toward \(H(3/4)\). Both changes are consistent with a stable equilibrium at \(3/4\).

For stability analysis, a scalar function adapted to the equilibrium is often more useful. Relative entropy provides one such function for important replicator models.

7. One distribution, several mathematical interpretations

We can now view the same vector \(\mathbf p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\) in several ways:

InterpretationQuestionMathematical object
ProbabilityWhich outcomes can occur, and how often?\(\mathbf p\)
Dynamical systemHow do these probabilities change?\(\mathbf p'(t)\)
Information theoryHow uncertain is the next observation?\(H(\mathbf p)\)
Exponential normalizationHow do relative growth rates change the distribution?\(p_i(0)e^{f_it}/Z(t)\)

These interpretations do not compete; they answer different questions about the same states. That is the organizing principle behind the rest of the course.
