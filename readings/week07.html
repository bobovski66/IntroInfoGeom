Fisher Information and Local Geometry

The second-order expansion of relative entropy contains a positive quadratic form. This form defines the Fisher information metric, which assigns lengths and angles to tangent vectors and measures the speed of paths through a family of distributions.

Learning goals

Derive the Fisher metric from KL and score functions; compute lengths and distances on the Bernoulli interval; calculate inner products and angles on the categorical simplex; identify Fisher speed with fitness variance along replicator trajectories; and recognize how natural gradients and square-root coordinates arise.

1. The quadratic term is a metric

Recall our expansion for two nearby categorical distributions. Fix a probability vector \(\mathbf p\) with all entries positive, and perturb it by a small vector \(\mathbf h\) whose components sum to zero. Week 6 gave

\[D(\mathbf p\|\mathbf p+\mathbf h)=\frac12\sum_i\frac{h_i^2}{p_i}+O(\|\mathbf h\|^3).\]

The linear term vanishes. What remains to second order is a positive quadratic expression. Just as the ordinary squared length of a vector is \(h_1^2+\cdots+h_n^2\), we can define a new squared length that depends on the base distribution.

Fisher inner product\[\boxed{g_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=\sum_i\frac{u_iv_i}{p_i},\qquad \sum_i u_i=\sum_i v_i=0.}\]

In particular, \(\|\mathbf u\|_F^2=g_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf u,\mathbf u)\). The normalization condition \(\sum_i u_i=0\) ensures that a velocity remains in the normalization plane \(\sum_i p_i=1\). For a positive distribution, this quadratic form is positive for every nonzero tangent vector.

We call it a Riemannian metric on the interior of the probability simplex. Here that means: at each probability distribution, we have an inner product on the possible infinitesimal motions away from that distribution.

Local, not global: KL divergence is not itself a distance, but its second-order expansion supplies the infinitesimal squared length. To measure the length of an entire curve, we must integrate the speed along it.

2. The Bernoulli Fisher metric

For a Bernoulli state write \(P_p=(p,1-p)\). An infinitesimal change in p moves the two probabilities in opposite directions, so the displacement is \(dP=(dp,-dp)\). Its squared Fisher length is

\[\begin{aligned}ds^2&=\frac{(dp)^2}{p}+\frac{(-dp)^2}{1-p}\\&=\boxed{\frac{dp^2}{p(1-p)}.}\end{aligned}\]

The coefficient \(g(p)=1/[p(1-p)]\) is not constant. At \(p=1/2\), it equals 4, whereas at \(p=1/10\), it equals \(100/9\approx11.11\). This means that an equal, sufficiently small change of probability has a larger Fisher length near a boundary than at the center.

The metric measures local statistical distinguishability. Near a distribution that nearly rules out one outcome, assigning that rare outcome a slightly different probability changes the predicted log likelihood more strongly than an equally sized probability displacement near an even split.

3. Fisher information from likelihood derivatives

We can arrive at the same metric without starting from KL. Let \(X\) be a random variable from a family of discrete distributions \(P_\theta\). For a possible observation x, the score is the derivative of its log probability:

\[S_\theta(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln P_\theta(X=x).\]

The Fisher information is the expected square of that score:

\[\boxed{I(\theta)=E_\theta[S_\theta(X)^2].}\]

In the Bernoulli family, when we use \(\theta=p\),

\[S_p(1)=\frac1p,\qquad S_p(0)=-\frac1{1-p}.\]

Taking the weighted average of the squares produces

\[\begin{aligned}I(p)&=p\left(\frac1p\right)^2+(1-p)\left(\frac{-1}{1-p}\right)^2\\&=\frac1p+\frac1{1-p}=\boxed{\frac1{p(1-p)}.}\end{aligned}\]

We have obtained precisely the same coefficient as before. Fisher information can thus be read as either the curvature of KL divergence at equality or the average sensitivity of log probabilities to the parameter.

For families depending smoothly on several parameters \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_m\), the Fisher information matrix has entries

\[G_{ab}(\boldsymbol\theta)=E_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\left[\frac{\partial\ln p(X;\boldsymbol\theta)}{\partial\theta_a}\frac{\partial\ln p(X;\boldsymbol\theta)}{\partial\theta_b}\right].\]

Its quadratic form \(d\boldsymbol\theta^T G\,d\boldsymbol\theta\) measures the squared length of a small parameter change, provided the parametrization identifies distributions locally without redundant directions.

4. Length and distance on the Bernoulli interval

A metric turns the idea of curve length into a calculus problem. If \(p(t)\) is an interior Bernoulli path, its Fisher speed is

\[\left\|\frac{dP}{dt}\right\|_F=\frac{|p'(t)|}{\sqrt{p(t)(1-p(t))}}.\]

Thus the length of the path between times \(t_0\) and \(t_1\) is

\[L_F=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{|p'(t)|}{\sqrt{p(t)(1-p(t))}}\,dt.\]

For monotone travel between probabilities a and b, this reduces to a single definite integral:

\[d_F(a,b)=\left|\int_a^b\frac{dp}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}\right|.\]

Substitute \(p=\sin^2(\theta/2)\), so \(\theta=2\arcsin\sqrt p\). Then \(dp=\frac12\sin\theta\,d\theta\) and \(\sqrt{p(1-p)}=\frac12\sin\theta\). The metric becomes

\[\boxed{ds^2=d\theta^2,\qquad\theta=2\arcsin\sqrt p.}\]

Consequently, the intrinsic distance between two Bernoulli distributions is

\[\boxed{d_F(a,b)=2\left|\arcsin\sqrt b-\arcsin\sqrt a\right|.}\]

This is an actual distance: nonnegative, symmetric, and satisfying the triangle inequality. We obtained it by measuring lengths with the Fisher metric, not by declaring KL divergence to be a metric. The two boundaries are not points of the positive-distribution manifold, but they are at finite total Fisher distance: the entire interval has length \(\pi\).

Worked example: two equal probability changes

Consider the moves from 0.10 to 0.15 and from 0.50 to 0.55. Both have ordinary coordinate length 0.05. Their Fisher lengths are

\[L_1=2(\arcsin\sqrt{0.15}-\arcsin\sqrt{0.10}),\qquad L_2=2(\arcsin\sqrt{0.55}-\arcsin\sqrt{0.50}).\]

Try them in the demo. The first is longer in the Fisher metric because the metric coefficient is larger toward the boundary.

5. Tangent vectors, lengths, and angles on the simplex

In the three-strategy model, let \(\mathbf p=(x,y,z)\) with \(z=1-x-y\). A tangent displacement obeys \(dz=-dx-dy\). Inserting this into the Fisher metric yields

\[\begin{aligned}ds^2&=\frac{dx^2}{x}+\frac{dy^2}{y}+\frac{(dx+dy)^2}{z}\\&=\begin{pmatrix}dx&dy\end{pmatrix}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\frac1x+\frac1z&\frac1z\\[3pt]\frac1z&\frac1y+\frac1z\end{pmatrix}}_{G(x,y)}\begin{pmatrix}dx\\dy\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\]

This matrix is symmetric and positive definite in the interior. The matrix gives the inner product of tangent vectors that change proportions while preserving their sum.

Worked example: Fisher and Euclidean angles differ

At \(q=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\), consider tangent vectors

\[\mathbf u=(0.1,-0.05,-0.05),\qquad\mathbf v=(0,0.1,-0.1).\]

Ordinarily their dot product is zero, so their Euclidean angle is \(90^\circ\). Under the Fisher metric,

\[g_q(u,v)=\frac{(0.1)(0)}{0.5}+\frac{(-0.05)(0.1)}{0.3}+\frac{(-0.05)(-0.1)}{0.2}=\frac1{120}.\]

Using their Fisher norms gives

\[\cos\alpha_F=\frac{g_q(u,v)}{\sqrt{g_q(u,u)g_q(v,v)}}=\frac17.\]

Therefore \(\alpha_F=\arccos(1/7)\approx81.8^\circ\). The underlying vectors have not changed; only the rule for measuring their angle has.

6. The speed of a replicator trajectory is fitness variance

For a smooth curve of distributions, the squared Fisher speed is

\[\|\mathbf p'(t)\|_F^2=\sum_i\frac{(p_i')^2}{p_i}.\]

Under replicator dynamics \(p_i'=p_i(f_i-\bar f)\), the expression simplifies:

\[\begin{aligned}\|\mathbf p'\|_F^2&=\sum_i\frac{p_i^2(f_i-\bar f)^2}{p_i}\\&=\sum_i p_i(f_i-\bar f)^2\\&=\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf p}(f).}\end{aligned}\]

This identity equates the squared Fisher speed of a replicator trajectory with the variance of fitness across the current distribution. If every type has the same fitness, there is no replicator motion and the Fisher speed is zero.

Worked example: two constant fitnesses

Suppose \(f_A=2,\ f_B=0\), with initial \(p_A=1/4,\ p_B=3/4\). Then \(\bar f=1/2\), and

\[\operatorname{Var}_p(f)=\frac14\left(2-\frac12\right)^2+\frac34\left(0-\frac12\right)^2=\frac34.\]

Meanwhile \(p_A'=p_A(2-\bar f)=3/8\), and the squared Fisher speed is \((3/8)^2/[(1/4)(3/4)]=3/4\). The two calculations agree exactly.

7. A first look at natural gradients

A gradient gives the direction of steepest increase of an objective relative to a chosen metric. The Fisher inner product therefore determines a gradient direction adapted to probability distributions.

For the Bernoulli interval, if \(\Phi(p)\) is a differentiable objective, the Fisher gradient is

\[\boxed{\operatorname{grad}_F\Phi=p(1-p)\,\frac{d\Phi}{dp}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p}.}\]

This simply means that the gradient is scaled by the inverse metric coefficient \(g^{-1}(p)=p(1-p)\). For a constant fitness advantage k, choose \(\Phi(p)=kp\). The corresponding Fisher-gradient flow is

\[p'=\operatorname{grad}_F\Phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p'=kp(1-p),\]

our logistic replicator equation once again! More generally, a two-strategy fitness difference \(\Delta(p)\) can be integrated to a potential \(\Phi'(p)=\Delta(p)\), giving the scalar replicator equation as a Fisher-gradient flow. In higher dimensions, a general fitness field need not arise from such a potential; this is a special but illuminating connection.

8. From local metric to the geometry of distributions

The quadratic expansion of KL divergence supplied tangent lengths and angles. Integrating the resulting Fisher speed produced an intrinsic distance along the Bernoulli family.

For the full simplex, we now have a Riemannian metric and can ask the usual geometric questions: What are the shortest paths between distributions? How is the simplex curved? Is there a coordinate system that makes the Fisher metric especially simple?

The change \(\theta=2\arcsin\sqrt p\) already hints at the answer. In Week 8 we'll explore square-root coordinates, discover a spherical picture for categorical distributions, and use it to understand geodesics in Fisher geometry.
