Square-Root Coordinates and Spherical Geometry

Last week, probability distributions acquired lengths and angles. Now a change of coordinates reveals an unexpected geometric object: the space of three-category distributions is a patch of an ordinary round sphere, and Fisher geodesics are great-circle arcs.

Simplex–sphere explorerGuided labWeekly quiz
Learning goals

Prove the square-root transformation is an isometry; derive spherical Fisher distance and great-circle interpolation; distinguish geodesics from linear mixtures; understand the metric boundary and completion; and recover the Bernoulli arc-length formula from the spherical picture.

1. A new map of the probability simplex

Recall the Fisher metric for a positive categorical distribution \(\mathbf p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\), restricted to tangent vectors \(\sum_i dp_i=0\):

\[ds_F^2=\sum_i\frac{dp_i^2}{p_i}.\]

Define new coordinates by taking positive square roots:

\[\boxed{q_i=2\sqrt{p_i}.}\]

Why the factor of two? Differentiate: \(dq_i=dp_i/\sqrt{p_i}\). Therefore

\[\boxed{\sum_i dq_i^2=\sum_i\frac{dp_i^2}{p_i}=ds_F^2.}\]

We have turned the Fisher quadratic form into the ordinary Euclidean quadratic form in q-coordinates. Meanwhile the normalization condition transforms into

\[\sum_iq_i^2=4\sum_i p_i=4.\]

So the image lies on a sphere of radius 2. Because probabilities are positive, each \(q_i\) is positive: we obtain the positive orthant of that sphere. The transformation is one-to-one, with inverse \(p_i=q_i^2/4\). It preserves every infinitesimal length and angle, and is therefore an isometry onto that spherical patch.

For three outcomes, the simplex interior is two-dimensional, just like the positive octant of a 2-sphere. The old triangle and the new spherical patch are different coordinate pictures of the same statistical manifold.

2. Great circles produce intrinsic shortest paths

A sphere has a familiar rule for shortest paths: between two non-antipodal points, the shorter arc of the great circle connecting them minimizes intrinsic length. A great circle is the intersection of the sphere with a plane through its center.

Take two interior probability distributions \(P=(p_i)\) and \(R=(r_i)\), and map them to the radius-2 vectors \(Q_i=2\sqrt{p_i}\) and \(S_i=2\sqrt{r_i}\). Let \(\alpha\) be their central angle. The dot-product formula gives

\[\cos\alpha=\frac{Q\cdot S}{4}=\sum_i\sqrt{p_ir_i}.\]

The angle is between 0 and \(\pi/2\), because these vectors have nonnegative coordinates. A radius-2 great-circle arc of angle \(\alpha\) has length \(2\alpha\). Thus

Fisher–Rao geodesic distance\[\boxed{d_F(P,R)=2\arccos\left(\sum_i\sqrt{p_ir_i}\right).}\]

The sum \(B(P,R)=\sum_i\sqrt{p_ir_i}\) is called the Bhattacharyya coefficient. By Cauchy–Schwarz, it lies between 0 and 1. It equals 1 precisely when P and R are equal. When the distributions have disjoint supports on the boundary, B is zero, and their distance in the metric completion is \(\pi\).

This is a true intrinsic distance. It is not KL divergence and is not the straight-line chord length in the surrounding Euclidean space.

3. The actual geodesic curve

Knowing the distance is useful, but we can also construct the shortest path itself. Let

\[Q=2\sqrt P,\qquad S=2\sqrt R,\qquad \alpha=\arccos\left(\sum_i\sqrt{p_ir_i}\right),\]

where square roots act coordinatewise. When \(P\ne R\), the constant-speed great-circle interpolation is

\[\boxed{Q(t)=\frac{\sin((1-t)\alpha)}{\sin\alpha}Q+\frac{\sin(t\alpha)}{\sin\alpha}S,\qquad0\leq t\leq1.}\]

Convert back to a probability distribution:

\[\boxed{p_i(t)=\left(\frac{Q_i(t)}2\right)^2.}\]

Every point on this curve stays normalized. The sine coefficients are nonnegative on this interval, so positive endpoints give a path that stays inside the positive orthant. If P and R are equal, the geodesic is simply constant.

The great-circle path has constant Fisher speed \(2\alpha=d_F(P,R)\) when parameterized over \(0\leq t\leq1\). That is why its length is exactly the distance formula above.

4. A straight mixture is not usually a geodesic

There is another perfectly valid path from P to R: the mixture or straight interpolation

\[M(t)=(1-t)P+tR.\]

This is a straight line in the conventional drawing of the simplex. However, its image \(2\sqrt{M(t)}\) is generally not a great-circle arc, so the mixture path usually has greater Fisher length than the Fisher geodesic.

We can calculate both lengths. A smooth probability curve \(c(t)\) has Fisher speed

\[\|c'(t)\|_F=\sqrt{\sum_i\frac{(c_i'(t))^2}{c_i(t)}}.\]

For the linear mixture, \(M_i'(t)=r_i-p_i\). Thus

\[\boxed{L_F(M)=\int_0^1\sqrt{\sum_i\frac{(r_i-p_i)^2}{(1-t)p_i+tr_i}}\,dt.}\]

The demo integrates this expression numerically and compares it with the analytic geodesic distance. For distinct interior states in a three-category simplex, we generally find \(L_F(M)>d_F(P,R)\). Special pairs may trace the same great-circle image with a different parameterization, so equality can occur.

5. The Bernoulli interval revisited

For two outcomes, write \(P_p=(p,1-p)\). The square-root transformation gives

\[Q_p=(2\sqrt p,\ 2\sqrt{1-p}).\]

This lies in the first quadrant of a circle of radius 2. The polar angle measured from the second coordinate axis is

\[\theta(p)=2\arcsin\sqrt p.\]

Thus the Fisher distance along this quarter-circle is

\[\boxed{d_F(p,r)=|\theta(p)-\theta(r)|=2|\arcsin\sqrt p-\arcsin\sqrt r|.}\]

That is exactly the formula we derived by integration in Week 7. The two calculations agree because they measure the same geometry in different coordinates.

The shortest path from one Bernoulli state to another is simply an arc of the circle. In ordinary p-coordinates its constant-speed parameterization is \(p(t)=\sin^2((1-t)\theta(p)/2+t\theta(r)/2)\).

6. What happens at the boundary?

The Fisher metric coefficient \(1/p_i\) becomes unbounded as a coordinate approaches zero. It might seem that the boundary must therefore be infinitely far away—but this conclusion is incorrect.

For the Bernoulli family, the total length from probability zero to one is \(\pi\), not infinity. On the square-root sphere, this is easy to see: the positive quadrant covers only a quarter-circle of radius 2, whose length is \(2(\pi/2)=\pi\).

For three-category distributions, the positive spherical octant likewise has boundary great-circle arcs at finite distance. We can complete the Fisher metric space by including probability vectors with zero components, corresponding to the closed positive octant of the radius-2 sphere. The Fisher metric is ordinarily defined only on the positive interior, but distances extend continuously to these limiting boundary states.

Worked example: pure categorical states

The pure distributions \((1,0,0)\) and \((0,1,0)\) belong to the metric completion, not the positive interior. Their square-root vectors \((2,0,0)\) and \((0,2,0)\) are perpendicular, hence subtend \(\pi/2\). Therefore

\[\boxed{d_F((1,0,0),(0,1,0))=\pi.}\]

By contrast, in Euclidean probability coordinates their distance is \(\sqrt2\), and \(D((1,0,0)\|(0,1,0))=+\infty\). These quantities measure fundamentally different things.

7. Curvature, chord distance, and distinguishability

The full 2-sphere of radius 2 has constant Gaussian curvature \(1/4\). Because square-root coordinates give an isometry, the two-dimensional interior of the three-category Fisher simplex has the same positive curvature.

This is a striking contrast with the flat triangle used for ordinary barycentric diagrams: the conventional picture gives convenient coordinates, but does not display the intrinsic Fisher curvature. For a two-outcome Bernoulli family, the state space is one-dimensional, so intrinsic Gaussian curvature is not defined; its spherical image is a circular arc.

The chord distance of two square-root vectors is also useful:

\[\begin{aligned}\|Q-S\|^2&=4\sum_i(\sqrt{p_i}-\sqrt{r_i})^2\\&=8\left(1-\sum_i\sqrt{p_ir_i}\right).\end{aligned}\]

It is related to the Hellinger distance. But remember that chord length differs from the spherical arc distance \(2\arccos B(P,R)\), except in the infinitesimal limit. The metric itself tells us which one measures shortest motion within the probability manifold.

Optional: curvature and higher-dimensional categorical families

For n categorical outcomes, the interior simplex has dimension \(n-1\) and maps isometrically to the positive orthant of the sphere \(S^{n-1}(2)\). If \(n\geq3\), every two-dimensional tangent plane has spherical sectional curvature \(1/4\), since the round sphere has radius 2. Nothing about the argument depends on restricting to three outcomes; we draw a two-dimensional simplex only because we can see it directly.

8. A new question: how should distributions move?

We've seen that probability distributions can be represented as points on a sphere, that Fisher distance is a spherical arc length, and that the Fisher metric determines which curves are shortest. But geodesics alone do not tell us how a population or learning system ought to evolve.

We already discovered that the replicator equation can sometimes be written as the gradient flow of a potential—provided the gradient is measured in Fisher geometry instead of the Euclidean geometry of probabilities. In Week 9 we will develop that idea systematically: natural gradients and potential-driven evolution.

The geometric lesson is now complete: information theory supplied KL divergence, KL supplied a metric, and the metric turned out to describe a round sphere under a simple coordinate map. Next we ask what that geometry does to dynamical systems.
