Week 4 · Reading

Probability Distributions as Mathematical States

In Week 3, our point on a triangle described a population. Now we will let a point describe the probabilities of any finite collection of outcomes, introduce numerical random variables, and study what changes when a distribution moves.

Distribution explorerGuided labQuiz
Learning goals

Distinguish a probability distribution from a random observation; compute expectations and variances; interpret the Bernoulli family and the categorical simplex; calculate tangent vectors and rates of change along curves of distributions; and compare exact probabilities with observed sample frequencies.

1. From population fractions to probability laws

Last week, \(\mathbf p=(p_A,p_B,p_C)\) described the fractions of three strategies in a population. Imagine selecting one individual at random. The chance of obtaining type A is \(p_A\), the chance of type B is \(p_B\), and the chance of type C is \(p_C\). The very same vector is now a probability distribution.

\[\boxed{p_i\geq0,\qquad \sum_i p_i=1.}\]

We can forget about population growth entirely. The outcomes might be weather categories, responses to a question, symbols in a communication channel, or three possible values recorded by a measuring instrument. The simplex records their probabilities regardless of what the outcomes mean.

Mathematical state

The distribution \((0.5,0.3,0.2)\) gives a complete set of probabilities for one categorical observation.

Realized observation

A sample produces one outcome—A, B, or C—not a probability vector. Repeated observations produce a data set.

This distinction will matter throughout the course: the distribution describes a model; the sample is what we see when we draw from that model.

2. Assign numerical values to outcomes

Suppose a random variable \(Z\) takes the values \(0,1,3\) with probabilities \(1/2,1/3,1/6\). We can organize its distribution in a table:

OutcomeABC
Numerical value \(z_i\)013
Probability \(p_i\)\(1/2\)\(1/3\)\(1/6\)

The outcomes A, B, and C are labels; the numbers \(0,1,3\) are values assigned to those labels. A random variable is precisely such an assignment. Notice that the probabilities must sum to one, but the numerical outcome values have no such restriction.

Two distributions can describe the same categories with different probabilities. Conversely, the same probability vector can be paired with different numerical outcome values, producing different expectations and variances.

3. Expectation and variance are functions of the distribution

The expected value is the probability-weighted average of the possible numerical outcomes:

\[\boxed{E_{\mathbf p}[Z]=\sum_i z_ip_i.}\]

For our example,

\[E[Z]=0\cdot\frac12+1\cdot\frac13+3\cdot\frac16=\frac56.\]

Expectation is a prediction about the long-run average under repeated independent sampling. An individual observation need not equal the expected value; indeed, \(5/6\) is not one of our possible outcomes.

Variance measures the expected squared deviation from the mean \(\mu=E[Z]\):

\[\operatorname{Var}(Z)=\sum_i p_i(z_i-\mu)^2=E[Z^2]-\mu^2.\]

Since \(E[Z^2]=0+\frac13+\frac{9}{6}=\frac{11}{6}\), we obtain

\[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(Z)=\frac{11}{6}-\left(\frac56\right)^2=\frac{41}{36}.}\]

Hold the values \(z_i\) fixed, and both the mean and variance become functions defined on the simplex. That is an elementary example of doing calculus on a space of probability distributions.

Worked example: two different probability states

Keep the outcomes \(z=(0,1,3)\), but compare \(P=(1/2,1/3,1/6)\) with \(Q=(1/4,1/2,1/4)\). Then

\[E_P[Z]=\frac56,\qquad E_Q[Z]=\frac54.\]

Both distributions assign probability to all three outcomes, but Q shifts more weight toward the larger numerical values, increasing the expectation. The possible outcomes themselves have not changed.

4. The Bernoulli family: a one-dimensional statistical space

When the outcomes are 0 and 1, a single number \(p=P(X=1)\) determines the entire distribution:

\[P_p=(1-p,p),\qquad 0\leq p\leq1.\]

This is the Bernoulli family. It is geometrically an interval, just like the two-strategy population model in Week 1. Its mean and variance are especially simple:

\[\boxed{E[X]=p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p).}\]

To derive the variance, note that \(X^2=X\) when \(X\) is either 0 or 1. Thus \(E[X^2]=E[X]=p\), and

\[\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-E[X]^2=p-p^2.\]

The variance is zero for a certain outcome, and maximized when both outcomes are equally likely. The quadratic factor \(p(1-p)\) is the same factor that appeared in our first replicator equation. For now, it has a different interpretation; later, we will discover why it also appears in the geometry of Bernoulli probabilities.

5. The categorical family is a triangle

For three categories, a distribution is specified by \((p_A,p_B,p_C)\) with nonnegative coordinates summing to one. All these distributions together make the simplex \(\Delta^2\).

We can use the first two probabilities as independent coordinates:

\[\mathbf p(x,y)=(x,y,1-x-y),\qquad x\geq0,\ y\geq0,\ x+y\leq1.\]

Suppose again \(z=(0,1,3)\). Then the expectation is a function on this triangular domain:

\[\mu(x,y)=0x+y+3(1-x-y)=3-3x-2y.\]

Its partial derivatives are \(\partial\mu/\partial x=-3\) and \(\partial\mu/\partial y=-2\). This already looks like familiar multivariable calculus: a scalar function defined on a two-dimensional domain.

For a smooth curve \(\mathbf p(t)\), normalization implies

\[\frac{d}{dt}(p_A+p_B+p_C)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_A'+p_B'+p_C'=0.\]

The derivative vector is tangent to the simplex. We encountered the same equation in the replicator model, but it applies to any differentiable path of normalized distributions.

6. Move between probability distributions

We can build a simple curve between two states without needing a differential equation. Let

\[P_0=(0.6,0.3,0.1),\qquad P_1=(0.2,0.3,0.5).\]

For \(0\leq t\leq1\), define linear interpolation:

\[\boxed{P(t)=(1-t)P_0+tP_1=(0.6-0.4t,\ 0.3,\ 0.1+0.4t).}\]

The velocity is constant:

\[\boxed{P'(t)=P_1-P_0=(-0.4,0,0.4).}\]

As expected, its coordinates sum to zero. The path remains in the simplex because it is a convex combination of two distributions. More generally, a curve of distributions is just a curve in the simplex; it need not be generated by the replicator equation.

For fixed numerical outcomes \(z_i\), expectation evolves according to the ordinary chain rule:

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}E_{P(t)}[Z]=\sum_i z_ip_i'(t).}\]

With \(z=(0,1,3)\), our path satisfies \(d\mu/dt=0(-0.4)+1(0)+3(0.4)=1.2\). Thus \(\mu(t)=0.6+1.2t\), increasing linearly from 0.6 to 1.8.

The variance generally does not vary linearly along the same path, because it contains the square of the changing mean. This is one of the simplest examples of two different scalar functions on the space of probability states.

7. A distribution is not the same as a sample

Imagine drawing independently from a fixed three-category distribution \(\mathbf p\). After \(n\) observations, let \(n_A,n_B,n_C\) count the outcomes. The empirical distribution is

\[\widehat{\mathbf p}=\left(\frac{n_A}{n},\frac{n_B}{n},\frac{n_C}{n}\right).\]

Its components also sum to one, so a sample gives another point on the simplex. But it need not equal the model distribution.

For example, even if the true distribution is \((1/2,1/3,1/6)\), a sample of 60 might contain 27 A, 21 B, and 12 C. The resulting empirical distribution is \((0.45,0.35,0.20)\).

As the number of independent observations becomes large, empirical proportions tend toward the underlying probabilities (the law of large numbers). A large sample is usually a more reliable description of the generating distribution than a tiny sample. It is still a sample, not the exact distribution itself.

8. Preparing to measure information

We now have two complementary objects: the space of possible distributions and the observations obtained by sampling from them. We can describe curves in that space and differentiate functions such as expectation and variance along those curves.

But we haven't yet answered a crucial question: how different are two probability distributions? A shift from \(p=0.49\) to \(p=0.50\) does not feel the same as a shift from \(p=0.01\) to \(p=0.02\), even though both are changes of 0.01. To study differences and distinguishability, we will need information theory and eventually a geometry adapted to probabilities.

Next week we begin with Shannon entropy and log-odds. Later, relative entropy will give us the Fisher metric by a Taylor expansion. The geometry will arise from the statistics, not from an abstract definition imposed at the beginning.
