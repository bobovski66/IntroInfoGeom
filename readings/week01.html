Competition, Proportions, and the Replicator Equation

A differential equation tells us how a state changes. Here the state is the fraction of a population belonging to one of two competing types.

Interactive demoGuided labQuiz
Learning goals

Derive the equation for a proportion; locate and classify equilibria; connect a phase line, a vector-field graph, and solution curves; and explain stable coexistence.

1. Why study proportions?

Let \(N_A(t)\) and \(N_B(t)\) denote the sizes of two populations. If their per-capita growth rates are \(f_A\) and \(f_B\), we may model their absolute sizes by

\[N_A'=f_A N_A,\qquad N_B'=f_B N_B.\]

We might be more interested in which type is becoming more common than in how large the total population is. Define

\[x=\frac{N_A}{N_A+N_B},\qquad 1-x=\frac{N_B}{N_A+N_B}.\]

Because these are proportions, \(0\leq x\leq 1\). The closed interval is our state space: \(x=0\) means only B is present; \(x=1\) means only A is present.

2. Deriving the replicator equation

Write \(N=N_A+N_B\). Apply the quotient rule and substitute the population equations:

\[\begin{aligned}x'&=\frac{N_A'N-N_A N'}{N^2}\\&=\frac{f_A N_A(N_A+N_B)-N_A(f_A N_A+f_B N_B)}{(N_A+N_B)^2}\\&=\frac{N_AN_B}{(N_A+N_B)^2}(f_A-f_B).\end{aligned}\]

Now \(N_A/N=x\) and \(N_B/N=1-x\). We obtain

Two-strategy replicator equation\[\boxed{x'=x(1-x)(f_A-f_B).}\]

If A has the higher growth rate, then \(x'>0\) in the interior. If B has the higher rate, then \(x'<0\). The factor \(x(1-x)\) makes the change vanish when either type is absent.

Direction

The sign of \(f_A-f_B\) tells us which proportion increases.

Speed

The factor \(x(1-x)\) makes the change slower near either endpoint, even for a fixed fitness difference.

3. Constant advantage: a logistic equation

If the fitness difference \(f_A-f_B=k>0\) is constant, we have

\[x'=kx(1-x).\]

There are equilibria at \(x=0\) and \(x=1\). Every interior solution moves right on the phase line.

Separation of variables recovers the familiar logistic solution for \(0<x_0<1\):

\[x(t)=\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{1-x_0}{x_0}\right)e^{-kt}}.\]

Here the limit \(x\to1\) means complete relative dominance of A, rather than a limit on total population size.

4. When fitness depends on frequency

Suppose the growth rates are \(f_A(x)=3-2x\) and \(f_B(x)=2x\). The advantage changes sign at the intersection \(3-2x=2x\), namely \(x=3/4\).

The replicator equation becomes

\[\boxed{x'=x(1-x)(3-4x).}\]

Its equilibria are \(0,3/4,1\). A sign chart gives \(x'>0\) for \(0<x<3/4\) and \(x'<0\) for \(3/4<x<1\):

6. The linearization explains how quickly we settle

Let \(F(x)=x(1-x)(3-4x)=3x-7x^2+4x^3\). Its derivative is

\[F'(x)=3-14x+12x^2,\qquad F'(3/4)=-3/4.\]

Near equilibrium, write \(x=3/4+u\). Taylor approximation gives

\[u'\approx-\frac34u,\qquad u(t)\approx u(0)e^{-3t/4}.\]

The negative derivative implies local asymptotic stability: small departures decay approximately exponentially. At the boundary equilibria \(0\) and \(1\), the derivatives are positive, consistent with their instability relative to interior states.

Worked example

For \(x'=x(1-x)(2-5x)\), the equilibria are \(0,2/5,1\). The vector field is positive below \(2/5\) and negative above it, so \(2/5\) is stable. By the product rule at this interior root,

\[F'(2/5)=\left(\frac25\right)\left(\frac35\right)(-5)=-\frac65.\]

Thus nearby perturbations decay approximately like \(e^{-6t/5}\).

7. Why the interval is invariant

Since \(x'=0\) at \(x=0\) and \(x=1\), these are constant solutions. For smooth growth rates, uniqueness prevents an interior solution from crossing either constant solution in finite time. The interval \([0,1]\) is therefore invariant under the differential equation's flow.

Finally, notice that \((x,1-x)\) can also be interpreted as a probability distribution: sample one individual, and \(x\) is the probability of encountering type A. This is our bridge to the probability simplex and information theory later in the course.

Check your understanding: stable versus stationary

At \(x=0.3\), the model \(x'=x(1-x)(3-4x)\) has \(x'>0\), so the state is moving. It tends toward the stationary state \(x=3/4\). An attracting equilibrium is stationary; nearby trajectories need not be.
