Gaussian Families and Hyperbolic Geometry

Our probability simplices turned into spherical geometry. What happens if outcomes vary continuously rather than belonging to a finite list? For the family of normal distributions, the Fisher metric reveals a different classical geometry: the negatively curved hyperbolic plane.

Gaussian geometry explorerGuided labFinal weekly quiz
Learning goals

Compute Fisher information from a Gaussian likelihood; interpret the metric on the mean–standard-deviation plane; transform it to the upper-half-plane model; identify hyperbolic geodesics and distances; distinguish KL divergence from Fisher distance; and synthesize how information geometry connects probability and dynamics.

1. A two-parameter family of continuous distributions

Up to now, we have described probabilities by vectors \(\mathbf p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\). The probability simplex is finite-dimensional because there are finitely many outcomes. Another way to obtain a finite-dimensional family is to restrict the shape of a continuous distribution while allowing its parameters to change.

Our example is the normal, or Gaussian, family, with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma>0\):

\[\boxed{p(x;\mu,\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).}\]

Each point \((\mu,\sigma)\) in the half-plane \(\sigma>0\) represents an entire probability density. The mean moves its center; the standard deviation changes its spread. This makes the parameter space a two-dimensional manifold of probability distributions.

The earlier course studied an equation for the evolution of a distribution. Here we first study the geometry of the space of possible distributions, before assigning any dynamics.

2. Calculate the Fisher information matrix

Recall Week 7's score: the derivative of a log probability with respect to a parameter. For a continuous family, we use the same definition, but compute expectations by integration.

Take the logarithm of the Gaussian density:

\[\ell(x;\mu,\sigma)=-\ln\sigma-\frac12\ln(2\pi)-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.\]

The two score components are

\[\boxed{\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu}=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^2},\qquad\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac1\sigma+\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{\sigma^3}.}\]

The Fisher information matrix records the expected products of these scores:

\[G_{ab}=E_{\mu,\sigma}\left[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta_a}\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta_b}\right],\qquad\boldsymbol\theta=(\mu,\sigma).\]

Write \(Y=X-\mu\). The Gaussian moments we need are \(E[Y]=0\), \(E[Y^2]=\sigma^2\), \(E[Y^3]=0\), and \(E[Y^4]=3\sigma^4\). They give

\[\begin{aligned}G_{\mu\mu}&=\frac{E[Y^2]}{\sigma^4}=\frac1{\sigma^2},\\G_{\mu\sigma}&=E\left[\frac{Y}{\sigma^2}\left(-\frac1\sigma+\frac{Y^2}{\sigma^3}\right)\right]=0,\\G_{\sigma\sigma}&=E\left[\left(-\frac1\sigma+\frac{Y^2}{\sigma^3}\right)^2\right]=\frac2{\sigma^2}.\end{aligned}\]

Thus the Fisher information matrix for the mean and standard deviation parameters is

\[\boxed{G(\mu,\sigma)=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{-2}&0\\[2pt]0&2\sigma^{-2}\end{pmatrix}.}\]

The off-diagonal entries vanish in these coordinates: the mean and standard-deviation score directions are Fisher-orthogonal. This conclusion depends on the coordinate choice; the geometry itself is coordinate-independent.

3. The Fisher line element is hyperbolic

Turn the matrix into the squared infinitesimal length of a parameter change:

\[\boxed{ds_F^2=\frac{d\mu^2+2\,d\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}.}\]

We already see that changing either parameter becomes more expensive as the distribution narrows. But the two directions have different coefficients. Introduce

\[u=\frac{\mu}{\sqrt2},\qquad v=\sigma>0.\]

Because \(d\mu=\sqrt2\,du\) and \(d\sigma=dv\),

\[\boxed{ds_F^2=2\,\frac{du^2+dv^2}{v^2}.}\]

The expression \((du^2+dv^2)/v^2\) is the standard metric on the Poincaré upper half-plane, a classical model of hyperbolic geometry. Our Gaussian Fisher metric is twice that standard hyperbolic metric. Multiplying a metric by 2 scales all lengths by \(\sqrt2\) and divides Gaussian curvature by 2.

\[\boxed{\text{Gaussian Fisher curvature }K=-\frac12.}\]

Contrast that with the categorical Fisher geometry of Week 8: a radius-2 sphere has positive curvature \(K=+1/4\). Both metrics arose by applying the same information-theoretic construction to different statistical families.

4. Geodesics in the upper half-plane

Week 8 showed that geodesics minimize intrinsic length. In the Poincaré upper half-plane, they are easy to recognize geometrically:

\[\text{Geodesics: vertical lines or semicircles orthogonal to }v=0.\]

Why do paths bend upward? Horizontal motion at height v has Fisher line element \(\sqrt2\,|du|/v\); it costs less at large v. A shortest path between two horizontally separated Gaussian distributions can therefore climb to a larger standard deviation, move across, then descend again.

When the means agree (\(\mu_1=\mu_2\)), the geodesic is vertical. Its distance follows by a single integral:

\[\boxed{d_F((\mu,\sigma_1),(\mu,\sigma_2))=\sqrt2\left|\ln\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}\right|.}\]

Otherwise the geodesic is an arc of a Euclidean circle whose center lies on the boundary v=0. A constant-speed parameterization is more subtle than simply interpolating the circle's polar angle; the explorer uses an exact hyperbolic interpolation.

5. A distance formula for any two Gaussians

For points \((u_1,v_1)\) and \((u_2,v_2)\) in the usual hyperbolic upper half-plane, the hyperbolic distance \(\delta\) satisfies

\[\cosh\delta=1+\frac{(u_1-u_2)^2+(v_1-v_2)^2}{2v_1v_2}.\]

Since the Gaussian Fisher metric is twice the hyperbolic metric, \(d_F=\sqrt2\,\delta\). Returning to \((\mu,\sigma)\)-coordinates yields

Exact Gaussian Fisher–Rao distance\[\boxed{d_F=\sqrt2\,\operatorname{arcosh}\left(1+\frac{(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2+2(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2}{4\sigma_1\sigma_2}\right).}\]

Worked example: changing spread without moving the mean

Compare \(N(0,1^2)\) and \(N(0,2^2)\). From the vertical formula,

\[d_F=\sqrt2\ln2\approx0.9803.\]

Doubling the standard deviation from 2 to 4 has the same Fisher distance: it is the ratio of spreads that matters along a vertical geodesic, rather than their arithmetic difference.

6. Geodesics are not straight parameter interpolation

Choose two normal distributions \(P=N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\) and \(R=N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\). A simple way to move between them is to interpolate the parameters:

\[\mu(t)=(1-t)\mu_1+t\mu_2,\qquad \sigma(t)=(1-t)\sigma_1+t\sigma_2.\]

These intermediate points are all valid Gaussians as long as \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2>0\). But a straight parameter segment is generally not a Fisher geodesic. Its Fisher speed is

\[\|\dot\gamma(t)\|_F=\frac{\sqrt{(\mu_2-\mu_1)^2+2(\sigma_2-\sigma_1)^2}}{(1-t)\sigma_1+t\sigma_2}.\]

Integrate to obtain the Fisher length of this straight segment. It is at least the geodesic distance; when the means agree it traces the same vertical geodesic with a nonconstant speed. When the means differ, the true geodesic generally bends.

In the demo, move two Gaussian states around the upper half-plane and compare a straight parameter segment with the true hyperbolic geodesic. The geodesic takes the shortest path in the Fisher metric—not necessarily in the Euclidean drawing.

7. Relative entropy, curvature, and the boundary

We also know KL divergence from Weeks 6–7. For two Gaussians \(P=N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\) and \(R=N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\), their directed divergence is

\[\boxed{D(P\|R)=\ln\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}+\frac{\sigma_1^2+(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}-\frac12.}\]

Reverse the arguments and the result generally changes. The Fisher distance, by contrast, is symmetric. For infinitesimally close Gaussians, KL's quadratic approximation recovers the Fisher metric; over long distances, these are different measurements.

There is another remarkable contrast with the finite categorical simplex. In Weeks 7–8, the simplex boundary lies at finite Fisher distance. In the Gaussian family, \(\sigma=0\) is infinitely far away along a vertical path:

\[\lim_{\sigma\to0^+}\sqrt2\left|\ln\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_0}\right|=+\infty.\]

Similarly, \(\sigma\to\infty\) is infinitely distant along a vertical path. The full upper half-plane is geodesically complete, whereas the interior categorical Fisher simplex is not. The positive and negative curvatures reflect genuinely different geometries of statistical families.

Optional: changing mean and scale together

In upper-half-plane coordinates, a simultaneous transformation \((u,v)\mapsto(au+b,av)\) with \(a>0\) preserves the metric \(2(du^2+dv^2)/v^2\). In Gaussian coordinates this reads \((\mu,\sigma)\mapsto(a\mu+\sqrt2b,a\sigma)\). So translating all means equally or rescaling both mean and standard deviation does not change pairwise Fisher distances. This is a useful symmetry of the Gaussian information geometry.

8. The course in one geometric narrative

We began by watching fractions change under differential equations. To understand those fractions, we studied the probability simplex. Then surprisal, entropy, and relative entropy led us to the Fisher metric, which turned probability distributions into objects with tangent vectors, lengths, and geodesics. Natural gradients showed how that geometry changes dynamics.

These examples show that geometry is not imposed arbitrarily on probability: the metric is recovered by asking how distinguishable nearby distributions are. Once we measure their differences, familiar geometric structures emerge naturally.
