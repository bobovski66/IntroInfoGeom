Natural Gradients and Potential-Driven Evolution

For a scalar objective on probability space, the direction of steepest increase depends on how tangent vectors are measured. The Fisher metric defines a natural gradient, and the resulting gradient flows include a familiar class of replicator equations.

Learning goals

Define the metric gradient and derive the natural-gradient formula; identify potential replicator systems; prove the ascent identity; connect constant fitness with exponential normalization; construct flows generated by relative entropy and Shannon entropy; and recognize why cyclic replicator dynamics cannot be a strictly ascending gradient flow.

1. The direction of steepest ascent depends on length

For an ordinary differentiable scalar function \(\Phi(x)\), the derivative \(\Phi'(x)\) describes how the function changes when x moves. With Euclidean length \(ds^2=dx^2\), the gradient vector has component \(\Phi'(x)\).

But if squared length is measured using \(ds^2=g(x)\,dx^2\), the gradient must be defined using the new inner product. For any velocity v, the defining equation is

\[g_x(\operatorname{grad}_g\Phi,v)=d\Phi_x(v).\]

In one dimension this becomes \(g(x)(\operatorname{grad}_g\Phi)^x v=\Phi'(x)v\). Cancelling v gives

\[\boxed{(\operatorname{grad}_g\Phi)^x=\frac{\Phi'(x)}{g(x)}.}\]

The metric converts the derivative (a covector) into the gradient (a tangent vector). The result is the direction of greatest increase for a unit vector in that metric, not necessarily for a unit Euclidean vector.

On the Bernoulli family we know \(g_F(x)=1/[x(1-x)]\), so

\[\boxed{\operatorname{grad}_F\Phi=x(1-x)\Phi'(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}.}\]

This is called a natural gradient: the gradient with respect to the Fisher information metric.

2. Our first replicator equation returns

Take the potential

\[\Phi(x)=3x-2x^2,\qquad\Phi'(x)=3-4x.\]

Its upward Fisher-gradient equation is

\[\boxed{x'=x(1-x)\Phi'(x)=x(1-x)(3-4x).}\]

This is the scalar replicator equation from Week 1. The interior equilibrium \(x^*=3/4\) is a strict local maximum of \(\Phi\), since \(\Phi''(x)=-4\), and interior trajectories move toward it.

Along any Fisher-gradient solution, the chain rule gives

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d\Phi}{dt}&=\Phi'(x)x'\\&=x(1-x)[\Phi'(x)]^2\\&=\boxed{x(1-x)(3-4x)^2\geq0.}\end{aligned}\]

The potential cannot decrease. In fact the derivative is strictly positive at every interior point that is not an equilibrium. The endpoints are stationary because of the factor \(x(1-x)\), but they lie on the boundary of the positive Bernoulli manifold.

Worked example: potential values

At \(x=0.5\), \(\Phi=1\). At \(x=0.75\), \(\Phi=9/8\). Along the interior solution from \(x(0)=0.1\), the potential increases toward \(9/8\). The flow is therefore both dynamically stable toward its interior equilibrium and an optimization flow for \(\Phi\).

3. Natural gradients on the full simplex

Let \(\Phi(\mathbf p)\) be a differentiable function on the interior of the n-category probability simplex. At a positive distribution \(\mathbf p\), tangent vectors satisfy \(\sum_i v_i=0\), and the Fisher metric is

\[g_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=\sum_i\frac{u_iv_i}{p_i}.\]

Write the ambient partial derivatives \(f_i=\partial\Phi/\partial p_i\), and define their probability-weighted mean \(\bar f=\sum_i p_if_i\). Consider

\[\boxed{(\operatorname{grad}_F\Phi)_i=p_i(f_i-\bar f).}\]

Why does this work? First, the components sum to zero:

\[\sum_i p_i(f_i-\bar f)=\sum_i p_if_i-\bar f\sum_i p_i=0.\]

Second, for any tangent vector \(\mathbf v\),

\[\begin{aligned}g_{\mathbf p}(\operatorname{grad}_F\Phi,\mathbf v)&=\sum_i(f_i-\bar f)v_i\\&=\sum_i f_iv_i-\bar f\sum_iv_i\\&=d\Phi_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf v).\end{aligned}\]

Thus it satisfies the defining gradient identity, and the Fisher metric is positive definite on tangent vectors, so this gradient is unique. We obtain

Simplex Fisher natural gradient\[\boxed{(\operatorname{grad}_F\Phi)_i=p_i\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p_i}-\sum_jp_j\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p_j}\right).}\]

Adding the same constant to all ambient partial derivatives does not change the result. This matters because the simplex lies in a normalization plane: only differences among those derivative components affect tangent motion.

4. Potential games, ascent, and information-geometric speed

Recall the general replicator equation \(p_i'=p_i(f_i-\bar f)\). The last section shows it is an upward Fisher-gradient flow whenever the fitnesses can be written as \(f_i=\partial_i\Phi\) for a potential \(\Phi\) on the simplex.

Along such a solution,

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d\Phi}{dt}&=d\Phi_{\mathbf p}(\mathbf p')\\&=g_{\mathbf p}(\operatorname{grad}_F\Phi,\mathbf p')\\&=\boxed{\|\operatorname{grad}_F\Phi\|_F^2\geq0.}\end{aligned}\]

From Week 7, the squared Fisher speed of a replicator trajectory is its current fitness variance. Therefore in a potential system we have the three-way identity

\[\boxed{\frac{d\Phi}{dt}=\|\mathbf p'\|_F^2=\sum_i p_i(f_i-\bar f)^2=\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf p}(f).}\]

This gives a reason for potential to increase: motion through Fisher geometry spends its speed on increasing the objective. It also makes clear why some replicator models can be interpreted as optimization problems.

However, a general state-dependent fitness field need not derive from a single scalar potential. Then the Fisher speed/variance identity still holds, but the potential-ascent identity need not.

5. Linear potentials give exponential normalization

One simple potential is \(\Phi(\mathbf p)=\sum_i c_i p_i\), where the constants \(c_i\) can be interpreted as strategy fitnesses. Its partial derivatives are \(f_i=c_i\), so the natural-gradient equation is

\[p_i'=p_i(c_i-\bar c).\]

We already solved this equation in Week 5:

\[\boxed{p_i(t)=\frac{p_i(0)e^{c_it}}{\sum_jp_j(0)e^{c_jt}}.}\]

So exponential normalization (softmax) is not merely an algebraic trick. It is the exact evolution of an upward Fisher-gradient flow for a linear potential. Its potential value increases at rate \(\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf p}(c)\).

For \(c=(2,1,0)\) starting at \(p(0)=(1/3,1/3,1/3)\), the gradient initially points in the direction \((1/3,0,-1/3)\). Strategy A gains probability, strategy C loses probability, and the mean fitness increases from 1 toward 2. The flow moves toward the boundary of the positive simplex as time increases.

Distinguish two questions: A Fisher geodesic is a shortest path between prescribed endpoints. A natural-gradient flow is determined by a scalar objective and a metric. Even with the same metric, the two paths generally do not coincide.

6. Relative entropy as a gradient-flow potential

Consider the KL divergence introduced in Week 6. Fix a positive reference distribution \(\mathbf q\), and define the potential

\[\Phi(\mathbf p)=-D(\mathbf q\|\mathbf p)=-\sum_iq_i\ln\frac{q_i}{p_i}.\]

Because the reference distribution is fixed,

\[f_i=\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p_i}=\frac{q_i}{p_i},\qquad\bar f=\sum_i p_i\frac{q_i}{p_i}=1.\]

The Fisher gradient simplifies dramatically:

\[\boxed{p_i'=p_i\left(\frac{q_i}{p_i}-1\right)=q_i-p_i.}\]

The explicit solution is

\[\boxed{\mathbf p(t)=\mathbf q+e^{-t}(\mathbf p(0)-\mathbf q).}\]

This is exponential contraction of the probability vector toward q along an ordinary straight mixture segment. Notice how the KL potential selected this particular dynamics, rather than the replicator fitnesses from Week 1.

Along the flow,

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}D(\mathbf q\|\mathbf p(t))=-\sum_i\frac{(q_i-p_i)^2}{p_i}\leq0.}\]

Here is an important synthesis: the same Fisher metric makes the KL gradient trajectory a straight mixture in p-coordinates, while Week 8's Fisher geodesic is generally curved in those coordinates. A natural-gradient trajectory does not have to be a geodesic.

7. Shannon entropy as a potential

Shannon entropy itself can also serve as an objective—but only for a dynamical system designed to increase it. Let

\[\Phi(\mathbf p)=H(\mathbf p)=-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i.\]

For interior distributions, \(f_i=-(\ln p_i+1)\). Since \(\bar f=-H(\mathbf p)-1\), the Fisher gradient gives

\[\boxed{p_i'=p_i[-\ln p_i-H(\mathbf p)].}\]

The entropy increases because this is its own upward gradient flow. To solve it, consider a log-ratio:

\[\frac{d}{dt}\ln\frac{p_i}{p_j}=f_i-f_j=-\ln\frac{p_i}{p_j}.\]

Thus all log-ratios decay exponentially. We obtain the normalized solution

\[\boxed{p_i(t)=\frac{p_i(0)^{e^{-t}}}{\sum_jp_j(0)^{e^{-t}}}.}\]

Every strictly positive initial distribution approaches the uniform distribution. Compare this with our Week 5 and Week 6 examples: Shannon entropy need not increase under an arbitrary replicator field, but it must increase under its own Fisher-gradient ascent.

8. Cyclic competition is not potential ascent

The cyclic three-strategy model from Week 3 provides a contrasting example. In the balanced rock–paper–scissors case, its fitness vector can be written

\[(f_A,f_B,f_C)=(p_B-p_C,\ p_C-p_A,\ p_A-p_B).\]

The mean fitness is zero, and the interior quantity \(K=p_Ap_Bp_C\) is conserved, because

\[\frac{d}{dt}\ln K=\sum_i\frac{p_i'}{p_i}=\sum_i f_i=0.\]

Nonstationary interior trajectories are closed orbits around \((1/3,1/3,1/3)\). Such a trajectory returns to its starting state. If it were an upward Fisher-gradient flow for a single-valued potential \(\Phi\), then we would have \(\dot\Phi=\|\mathbf p'\|_F^2>0\) throughout that nonstationary orbit. But \(\Phi\) cannot strictly increase and return to its original value. This is a contradiction.

The Fisher metric applies to both systems. What differs is the dynamical structure: potential-driven fields climb a scalar landscape, while a cyclic field can circulate along closed curves. Geometry gives us language and tools for both, without asserting that every dynamical system is an optimization algorithm.

The Fisher metric gives a geometric interpretation of potential-driven replicator dynamics. The same construction applies to families of continuous distributions, including the Gaussian family studied in the final chapter.
