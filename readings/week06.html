Relative Entropy, Stability, and the Geometry Ahead

Shannon entropy measures uncertainty in one distribution. But how do we measure the information lost when we use the wrong distribution? Relative entropy answers that question—and supplies a remarkably precise Lyapunov function for the replicator equation we have studied since Week 1.

Learning goals

Compute and interpret Kullback–Leibler divergence; recognize its directionality and behavior at zero probabilities; derive its nonnegativity; use it as a Lyapunov function for stable two-strategy replicator equations; interpret it as excess expected log loss; and find the local quadratic approximation that motivates the Fisher metric.

1. Uncertainty is not the same as mismatch

Last week, we met Shannon entropy \(H(P)=-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i\), the expected surprisal when observations come from P. Entropy does not require a second distribution. It measures uncertainty about an outcome drawn from P.

Suppose instead that observations really follow a distribution Q, but we predict them with another distribution P. If we assign probability \(p_i\) to outcome i, then the surprisal under our model is \(-\ln p_i\). The expected log loss when Q generates the outcomes is

\[\operatorname{CE}(Q,P)=-\sum_i q_i\ln p_i.\]

CE stands for cross-entropy. If we knew the correct distribution Q and used it for prediction, our expected log loss would be \(H(Q)=-\sum_iq_i\ln q_i\). Subtract them:

Kullback–Leibler divergence\[\boxed{D(Q\|P)=\operatorname{CE}(Q,P)-H(Q)=\sum_iq_i\ln\frac{q_i}{p_i}.}\]

The first argument Q is the reference or generating distribution; the second P is the model against which we compare it. Using P instead of Q incurs an excess expected logarithmic loss of exactly \(D(Q\|P)\) nats per observation.

Worked example: two Bernoulli states

Let \(Q=(3/4,1/4)\) and \(P=(1/2,1/2)\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}D(Q\|P)&=\frac34\ln\frac{3/4}{1/2}+\frac14\ln\frac{1/4}{1/2}\\&=\frac34\ln\frac32+\frac14\ln\frac12\\&\approx0.130812\text{ nats}.\end{aligned}\]

Reverse the order and we obtain \(D(P\|Q)\approx0.143841\) nats. The difference is real, not rounding error.

2. Relative entropy is not an ordinary distance

We know that identical distributions should have no mismatch, and indeed \(D(P\|P)=0\). KL divergence is nonnegative, but unlike a metric it is generally not symmetric and does not satisfy the usual triangle inequality.

In particular, \(D(Q\|P)\) and \(D(P\|Q)\) often have different sizes, and that matters when deciding which distribution is reference and which is approximation.

There is also an important boundary issue. If \(q_i>0\) but \(p_i=0\), then model P declares an outcome impossible even though Q assigns it positive probability. In that case \(D(Q\|P)=+\infty\). Terms with \(q_i=0\) contribute zero by continuity. In the interior of the probability simplex, all components are positive and both divergences are finite.

3. Why KL divergence is nonnegative

For positive probabilities, the elementary inequality \(\ln u\leq u-1\) holds for every \(u>0\), with equality only at \(u=1\). Apply it to \(u=p_i/q_i\):

\[-\ln\frac{p_i}{q_i}\geq1-\frac{p_i}{q_i}.\]

Multiply by \(q_i\), then sum over outcomes:

\[\begin{aligned}D(Q\|P)&=-\sum_iq_i\ln\frac{p_i}{q_i}\\&\geq\sum_i(q_i-p_i)=1-1=0.\end{aligned}\]

Equality requires \(p_i=q_i\) for every i. Thus KL divergence has a strict minimum of zero when the two distributions agree. The proof extends to boundary distributions using the conventions just described.

Geometric interpretation: On the simplex, fixing a reference distribution Q gives a scalar function \(P\mapsto D(Q\|P)\). Like a height function, it assigns a nonnegative value to each state, with its only minimum at Q. We can ask how that function changes along a dynamical trajectory.

4. Return to our stable coexistence equation

Our familiar replicator model is

\[x'=x(1-x)(3-4x),\qquad x^*=\frac34.\]

We already know that all interior trajectories approach the stable equilibrium \(x^*=3/4\). But Shannon entropy need not decrease or increase monotonically as the solution approaches that equilibrium. Let's instead measure the divergence from the equilibrium distribution to the current distribution:

\[\boxed{V(x)=D(x^*\|x)=\frac34\ln\frac{3/4}{x}+\frac14\ln\frac{1/4}{1-x}.}\]

Differentiate with respect to the current proportion x:

\[\begin{aligned}V'(x)&=-\frac{x^*}{x}+\frac{1-x^*}{1-x}\\&=\frac{x-x^*}{x(1-x)}.\end{aligned}\]

Apply the chain rule along a solution, then substitute \(3-4x=-4(x-x^*)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{dV}{dt}&=\frac{x-x^*}{x(1-x)}x'\\&=(x-x^*)(3-4x)\\&=\boxed{-4(x-x^*)^2.}\end{aligned}\]

The result is strictly negative whenever the interior solution is away from the equilibrium, and zero at the equilibrium itself. This is stronger than simply noting that the arrows point toward \(x^*\): the function V gives us a quantitative, continuously decreasing measure of mismatch along the flow.

5. Lyapunov functions and a family of stable systems

A Lyapunov function for an equilibrium is a scalar quantity minimized at that equilibrium and nonincreasing along trajectories nearby. A strictly decreasing function away from the equilibrium can establish asymptotic stability under appropriate regularity and compactness conditions.

We can generalize the previous calculation. Consider

\[x'=x(1-x)(a-bx),\qquad b>0,\qquad q=\frac ab\in(0,1).\]

The interior equilibrium is q. Define \(V(x)=D(q\|x)\). Then

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}D(q\|x(t))=(x-q)(a-bx)=-b(x-q)^2\leq0.}\]

This proves local asymptotic stability of q, and the phase-line analysis shows that every interior initial condition approaches q. The divergence is nonnegative, vanishes only at q, and decreases along every nonstationary interior trajectory.

Notice how the order of the KL arguments matters to this particularly simple derivative. The equilibrium distribution is the first argument and the evolving distribution is the second. The reverse divergence \(D(x\|q)\) is also decreasing in this particular scalar model, but its derivative has a different expression.

Optional: the multi-strategy KL derivative

For a fixed positive reference distribution \(\mathbf q\) and an interior replicator trajectory \(\mathbf p(t)\), we find

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dt}D(\mathbf q\|\mathbf p)&=-\sum_iq_i\frac{p_i'}{p_i}\\&=-\sum_iq_i(f_i-\overline f)\\&=\overline f-\sum_iq_if_i.\end{aligned}\]

Unlike the two-strategy coexistence example, this expression is not automatically nonpositive for every payoff matrix. Its sign depends on the fitness structure and the chosen reference. This is why Lyapunov functions must be justified for the specific system.

6. Visualize KL on a probability triangle

Take a reference categorical distribution \(Q=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\) and an initial model \(P_0=(0.15,0.20,0.65)\). The simplex contains both points. We can form the straight path

\[P(t)=(1-t)P_0+tQ,\qquad0\leq t\leq1.\]

As P moves toward Q along this path, KL divergence \(D(Q\|P(t))\) decreases. In fact, \(P\mapsto D(Q\|P)\) is convex in P for positive components, and has its minimum at Q. This interpolation is a simple experiment in a landscape of distributions, not a claim that every probability curve follows a replicator equation.

Using the expected log-score interpretation, the height \(D(Q\|P)\) tells us the extra average penalty for predicting using P rather than Q. The heatmap in the demo displays that penalty across the triangle and lets you compare its asymmetrical reverse \(D(P\|Q)\).

7. A quadratic approximation appears

KL divergence is not a metric, but consider two nearby Bernoulli distributions. Fix \(0<q<1\) and write the current state as \(x=q+h\), where h is small enough to stay inside the interval. We can Taylor-expand

\[D(q\|q+h)=q\ln\frac{q}{q+h}+(1-q)\ln\frac{1-q}{1-q-h}.\]

At \(h=0\), the divergence is zero. Its first derivative is zero, and its second derivative is

\[\left.\frac{d^2}{dh^2}D(q\|q+h)\right|_{h=0}=\frac1q+\frac1{1-q}=\frac1{q(1-q)}.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{D(q\|q+h)=\frac{h^2}{2q(1-q)}+O(h^3).}\]

The coefficient depends on the location q. A small change near a boundary has a larger quadratic divergence than the same-sized change near \(q=1/2\). Probability space is therefore not measured uniformly by this local notion of distinguishability.

More generally, for a positive categorical reference \(\mathbf q\) and small tangent displacement \(\mathbf h\) satisfying \(\sum_ih_i=0\),

\[D(\mathbf q\|\mathbf q+\mathbf h)=\frac12\sum_i\frac{h_i^2}{q_i}+O(\|\mathbf h\|^3).\]

Here is the transition we've been working toward: the quadratic term supplies a positive-definite form on the tangent plane. In Week 7 we will recognize it as the Fisher information metric and begin studying lengths, angles, and geometry on statistical manifolds.

8. Four notions of a distribution's behavior

Mathematical quantityWhat it measures
\(H(P)\)Uncertainty about an outcome drawn from P
\(D(Q\|P)\)Information mismatch between Q and P
\(\frac{d}{dt}D(Q\|P(t))\)Change of mismatch along a chosen trajectory
\(\frac12\sum_i h_i^2/q_i\)Leading local quadratic cost of a displacement from Q

We began with phase lines and population fractions, and we've arrived at a local quadratic form on a space of probability distributions. Every step used tools from calculus, probability, and undergraduate differential equations. The next step is to make the resulting geometry explicit.
